My first authored paper with Dr. Liu tests whether human intuitive physical reasoning and goal directed attention rely on shared or distinct neural resources will be submitted for publication in the next few weeks.

**Research summary.** We treat institutions as special social groups. On a collective level, we speak as if the entire institution has a singular mind of its own (e.g. "the government wants..."). This singular mind ascription is so pervasive that we understand the people that make up an institution as _occupiers of roles_, with goals and actions in service of the overarching institution, rather than as individual agents with their own goals. For example, if we see someone stocking shelves at a supermarket, we assume they are doing so because their role at the institution–a worker at a supermarket–obligates them to stock shelves. We would be surprised if that individual was stocking shelves purely because they enjoy it. **I am interested in these unique ways we understand institutions, and how these unique properties are used to align people with-or-against institutions.** As central as the goals and beliefs of institutions are to our understanding of the agents that make up institutions (and their actions), the process of discerning the goals and beliefs of institutions is not obvious. How do we _know_ what an institution believes? Furthermore, when do these beliefs get co-opted in service of a greater coalition? For example, people often ascribe political stances to institutions (e.g. "universities are liberal" or "banks are conservative"). How do these co-opted representations form, persist, and grow? Ultimately, when do people align themselves with-or-against an institution, and how does this change the way we see the individual members that make up the institution?

In the real world, confidence in (almost all public) institutions reached [a 52 year low](https://news.gallup.com/poll/1597/confidence-institutions.aspx) in the United States. While a variety of political, economical, and historical reasons undoubtedly play a role in dwindling public perception of institutions, confidence in institutions still sinks lower year after year. The traditional method of studying institutions omits cognitive science perspectives. Perhaps this is the missing piece to turn around this downward spiral: if we can understand the way humans think about institutions, then maybe we can develop avenues to restore confidence in institutions.
